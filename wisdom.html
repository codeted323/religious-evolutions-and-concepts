Shared Wisdom

Moral and practical guidance from the wisdom texts of eleven religious traditions, lined up to show where they say the same thing and where they stand apart.

The passages come from the texts each tradition treats as practical guidance for living. That means sayings, proverbs, parables and counsel more than formal law: Pirkei Avot and Proverbs, the Sermon on the Mount and the parables of Jesus, the Quran and hadith, the Bhagavad Gita, Tirukkural and Mahabharata, the Dhammapada and the Buddha's advice to Sigala, the Jain sutras, the Guru Granth Sahib, the Dao De Jing, the Analects, the Zoroastrian Menog-i Khrad, and Bahá'u'lláh's Hidden Words.

The collection has 244 passages, grouped into 31 shared themes and 22 distinctive ones.

How to read it

Matched themes are ideas that at least three traditions in this collection express in substantially the same way. Each one lists which traditions it was found in. Where traditions qualify or disagree with the shared idea, a note says so.

Unmatched themes are teachings found in only one or two traditions here, or points where the traditions take different positions. "Unmatched" means not matched within this collection. These are large literatures, and a parallel may well exist in a text not sampled here.

Within each section, themes are grouped by area of life.

Quotations come from public-domain or freely available translations, which are named and linked under each passage. Longer verses are shortened, with "…" marking cuts. Text in [brackets] is either the translator's own insertion or a connecting phrase or paraphrase of mine. A few passages are given only in paraphrase, and are labelled as such. The theme summaries are mine.

Limits. This is a sample, not a census. The coverage reflects which texts could be checked against an online source, so a tradition missing from a theme means only that no matching passage was gathered, not that the tradition lacks the idea. Translations differ, and an English match can hide real differences in the original concepts.

Treating others

The Golden Rule

Found in 7 of 11 traditions: Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Confucianism, Zoroastrianism

Every tradition in this collection states some form of reciprocity. Several phrase it in the negative, as 'do not do to others what you would not want done to you' (Hillel, Confucius, the Mahabharata, the Tirukkural, the Zoroastrian Dadestan). Jesus and the hadith phrase it in the positive, as 'do' or 'love for your brother'. The Buddhist version rests on empathy: everyone fears violence, so put yourself in the other's place.

Leviticus 19:18 · JPS 1917, Sefaria

“That which is hateful to you do not do to another; that is the entire Torah, and the rest is its interpretation. Go study.”

Talmud, Shabbat 31a (Hillel) · William Davidson Edition, Sefaria

“whatever you desire for men to do to you, you shall also do to them; for this is the law and the prophets.”

“As you would like people to do to you, do exactly so to them.”

Luke 6:31 · World English Bible, eBible.org

Do no harm to living beings

Found in 4 of 11 traditions: Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism

Conflict, anger and enemies

Answer wrong with good

Found in 7 of 11 traditions: Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism, Daoism

Responding to hostility with kindness, rather than in kind, is one of the strongest cross-tradition matches. It appears in the Hebrew Proverbs, the Sermon on the Mount and Paul, the Quran, the Dhammapada, the Tirukkural, the Dao De Jing and Shaikh Farid in the Guru Granth Sahib.

Not every tradition agrees. Confucius, asked about repaying injury with kindness, says to repay injury with justice instead (see Unmatched → Repay injury with justice). The Quran (42:40) allows a proportionate response but holds pardon as better.

“If thine enemy be hungry, give him bread to eat, And if he be thirsty, give him water to drink”

Romans 12:17-21 · World English Bible, eBible.org

Non-resistance: turn the other cheek

Found in 2 traditions in this collection.

Jesus goes beyond not retaliating to not resisting at all, and offering the other cheek. The closest parallel in this collection is Shaikh Farid's verse in the Guru Granth Sahib: do not strike back, kiss their feet and go home.

Christianity

“don't resist him who is evil; but whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also.”

Matthew 5:39 · World English Bible, eBible.org

“Farid, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists. Kiss their feet, and return to your own home.”

Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1378 (Shalok Farid) · Guru Granth Sahib, tr. Sant Singh Khalsa, SriGranth.org

Repay injury with justice, not kindness

A point where the traditions disagree.

Confucius explicitly declines the idea of repaying injury with kindness. If you repay injury with kindness, he asks, what will you repay kindness with? This directly contrasts with Dao De Jing 63 and with the 'answer wrong with good' theme. The Quran takes a middle position.

Islam

“The guerdon of an ill-deed is an ill the like thereof. But whosoever pardoneth and amendeth, his wage is the affair of Allah.”

Quran 42:40 · Pickthall (1930), Quranic Arabic Corpus

Daoism

“to consider what is small as great, and a few as many; and to recompense injury with kindness. ... does things that would become great while they are small.”

Dao De Jing 63 · Legge, Satyori / Sacred Books of the East 39

Confucianism

“Recompense injury with justice, and recompense kindness with kindness.”

Analects 14.36 · Legge, MIT Internet Classics Archive
